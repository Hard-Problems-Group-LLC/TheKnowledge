UI Complexity Limits
====================

Guideline
---------
Treat roughly 7 to 9 items as the practical maximum size for one user
interface group, such as a menu section, tab bar, button cluster, or tile
set. If a group wants more than that, split it into meaningful subgroups and
add a child navigation surface such as a submenu, drill-down, or child page.

Why
---
Miller's 1956 paper is the origin of the familiar "seven, plus or minus two"
idea, but the paper is really about limits in immediate judgment and memory,
with heavy emphasis on chunking rather than a literal UI rule. Later work,
especially Cowan's review, argues that the effective core working-memory
limit may be closer to about four chunks. In practice, this means the exact
number is disputed, but the broader lesson is stable: large flat choice sets
become harder to scan, compare, remember, and revisit.

Current design guidance points in the same direction. Microsoft recommends
organizing related menu commands into groups of about seven or fewer items.
Apple recommends using submenus sparingly and only when a simpler menu cannot
work. Laws of UX also treats "7 +/- 2" as a heuristic, not a law, and notes
that people chunk information to reduce cognitive load.

Repository recommendation
-------------------------
- Use 7 to 9 items as a strong upper bound for one visible logical group.
- Prefer fewer items when choices are unfamiliar, visually dense, or require
  comparison.
- When you exceed the bound, split by task, object type, or workflow stage
  instead of appending more peers to the same surface.
- Do not add hierarchy mechanically. If a submenu or child page makes items
  harder to find, regroup the surface instead of making it deeper.

Brief theoretical note
----------------------
Rate-distortion theory offers a useful analogy here. When a screen asks a
person to compress too many alternatives into one quick decision, more signal
is lost and more distortion is introduced. Smaller, semantically coherent
groups reduce that loss by making the tradeoff between completeness and
comprehension more favorable.
